Encampment, Self-Settlement and the Recovery of Displaced Households

The same 391 families told me about their lives in Syria before the war and in Jordan in 2016. Inside Zaatari camp and outside it, Jordan hosted them under two different sets of rules. This is what each family lost, what it rebuilt, and why.

By Felipe H. Rodríguez Villalta · Original household survey, May to July 2016 · Analysis rebuilt and extended, September 2026

Chapter 1Three hundred and ninety-one households

Between May and July 2016 I interviewed one adult in each of 391 Syrian refugee households: 257 in the twelve districts of Zaatari camp, and 134 in Irbid, Ramtha, Jerash, Zarqa and the towns around them. The dots below stand for them, one dot per household.

391 households, one dot each, interviewed in the summer of 2016. Every household answered the same questionnaire twice over: about its life in Jordan, and about its life in Syria before the war.
257 lived in Zaatari camp and 134 outside it, in the towns of northern Jordan. Inside the camp: registration, food vouchers, a caravan and almost no work. Outside: movement, informal work and rent.
The 134 households outside the camp got there in different ways. 49 never entered a camp; 42 left Zaatari with a Jordanian sponsor, the legal route until 2015; 36 left informally; 7 left with a permit or did not say. How a family left later decided which documents it could hold.
The survey never asked about Ministry of Interior cards. It did ask about UNHCR registration: 15 households were still waiting to be registered, twelve of them outside the camp, most of them recent arrivals or families that never entered a camp. The ring marks them.
Who answered matters. Women answered 63 percent of the interviews outside the camp and 21 percent inside, the largest difference between the two samples. Every subjective answer on this page is read with that in mind. Hollow dots are women.
Asked how they had coped with a fall in income, 73 percent of households outside the camp said they had eaten less, against 55 percent inside. Households with more income cut food more often. The rest of this essay explains why, and what it would have taken to change it.

Chapter 2What they had, and what they have

Because every question was asked for Syria as well as for Jordan, the survey can be read as a two-period panel of the same household. Hollow marks are Syria before displacement; filled marks are Jordan in 2016.

Households reported their Syrian income in pounds, and the pound collapsed during the war. At one pre-crisis rate for everyone, camp households went from $465 a month to $220 and households outside from $495 to $384. Four in five camp households lost income; fewer than half outside did.
The thesis converted each family's income at the rate of the year it left Syria. Read that way camp households lose little and households outside gain. But nominal pound incomes are the same across departure cohorts, so this rate mostly records when a family left, not what it earned. It stays in the appendix.
At purchasing power parity, households outside the camp are back at their pre-war standard of living and camp households are at about 60 percent of theirs. The factors are provisional, but the ranking does not depend on them.
What does not move is the gap. Incomes in Syria were nearly identical (a standardised difference of 0.06), so the difference in within-household changes is about the same as the raw 2016 gap: $134 a month at the fixed rate, with a standard error of $61. The income advantage of households outside the camp was not inherited from Syria.

Almost every household owned a refrigerator and a stove in Syria. In Zaatari fewer than one in four has a stove and fewer than half a refrigerator, because the caravan came without a kitchen; outside the camp, ownership of both is back near Syrian levels. Counting four durable goods, camp households went from 3.3 items to 1.6 and households outside from 3.4 to 2.7.

Savings went the same way for everyone. Half of the households later found outside the camp had savings in Syria, against fewer than one in five of those who stayed; by 2016 both groups were at 2 percent. Debt rose from about a quarter of households to 60 percent in the camp and 69 percent outside, and since formal loans are nearly absent in Jordan, that debt is owed to shops, relatives and landlords.

Work collapsed and recovered unevenly. Nearly every household had an earner in Syria. In 2016, 39 percent of camp households did, mostly in incentive positions with NGOs and short weeks, against 64 percent outside, where those who work put in longer weeks than they did in Syria.

Chapter 3Who left the camp

Until 2015 the legal way out of Zaatari was a Jordanian sponsor, which took means and contacts. The pre-displacement module lets us see directly what separated the two groups before anyone crossed the border, and it looks exactly like that.

Income in Syria, home ownership, household size, the respondent's age and the year of arrival are balanced. What differs is liquidity and origin: households now outside the camp had savings and a car far more often, came from towns rather than villages, had finished secondary school more often, and came less often from Daraa.

Selection into leaving the camp ran through the ability to arrange an exit, not through earning capacity. That is the selection the analysis has to absorb, and the pre-displacement outcomes make the adjustment testable: whatever reweighting works should leave no difference on life in Syria.

The largest difference of all is who answered the questionnaire.

Chapter 4What living outside the camp did, once like is compared with like

Outcomes are grouped into seven domains, each an index in standard deviations of the camp and signed so that higher is better. Camp households are reweighted so that their pre-displacement means match those of out-of-camp households; the interval is 95 percent.

The raw differences are large in both directions: households outside the camp have more income, more assets and more work, and report much lower wellbeing, weaker social ties and less security.
After reweighting camp households to match on savings, car, urban origin, schooling, Daraa origin, household size, age, arrival year, business and income in Syria, the income and work advantages shrink by half and stop being distinguishable from zero. Part of the raw gap was who left the camp.
Three effects survive every estimator. Assets (+0.9 SD; a hidden factor would have to multiply the odds of leaving the camp more than sixfold to explain it), wellbeing and social ties (about −1.5 SD), and security (−0.9 SD, robust in sign but fragile to hidden bias). Comparable households outside the camp are also more often in debt.
The shaded band is the minimum effect the sample can detect, about 0.30 SD. With 134 treated households and an effective control sample of about 40 after reweighting, estimates inside the band say something about direction and little about size. The other estimators agree on the sign of every index except coping, where every estimate is close to zero.

Chapter 5Why: rent, a policy shock, registration and empty days

The thesis's most counterintuitive result was that the households with more income cut food more often. Three things explain it, and each of them could have been changed by a programme.

Households outside the camp earn almost twice as much as camp households and spend almost twice as much. Once rent is paid, the two groups are living on the same money: income after rent per person is 39 dinars outside and 36 in the camp, and food spending per person is 27 against 29. What differs is what that money has to cover. Camp households put three quarters of their spending into food because water, shelter, schooling and most health care come with the caravan; households outside pay for electricity, water, transport and school.

Assistance is not the difference either. By mid-2016, nine in ten households in both groups received some, WFP vouchers for most of them, and per person it came to about 19 dinars in the camp and 23 outside. What differed was what assistance was relative to the budget: three quarters of income in the camp, half of it outside.

The median household outside the camp pays 35 percent of its income in rent and a quarter pay more than 56 percent. Food cuts rise with that burden, from 59 percent of households in the lowest third to 86 percent in the highest. And the questionnaire asked why income had fallen: 89 of the 103 out-of-camp households that gave a reason named a reduction of assistance, the WFP cut of August 2015 that fell on refugees outside the camps and not on those inside; nine in ten of them had eaten less.

Put together, the raw gap of 18 points in food cuts between households outside and inside the camp falls to 10 once rent and income after rent are held fixed, and to 4 once the households that attributed their loss of income to the assistance cut are marked; those households were 27 points more likely to have cut food. The attribution is their own account, so that number dates the shock rather than measuring its effect. The result the thesis found puzzling was rent, and a policy shock that hit one group and not the other in the year before the survey. Both are things a programme could have changed.

Of the twelve out-of-camp households still awaiting registration, none received WFP vouchers and none received UNHCR cash, against 93 and 32 percent of registered refugees; a third received assistance of some other kind. Their incomes were no lower, but nine in ten cut food against seven in ten. Registration gated assistance, exactly as the documentation literature says, and the survey shows it with twelve households. Among the routes out of the camp, households that left informally report far lower personal integration (3.7 against 5.7 on a ten-point scale for sponsored households) and are the group most often without anyone to call in an emergency.

Among respondents without a job, 80 percent in the camp describe their day as nothing or sitting at home, against 37 percent outside. With employment and household characteristics held fixed, the camp adds about 41 points to the probability of an empty day. This is the quantitative counterpart of what the ethnographies of Zaatari and Azraq describe, and it varies inside the camp itself.

One result complicates the story. Within the camp, respondents with empty days report no less happiness or optimism than the others, and they report more personal security (4.3 against 3.7 on a five-point scale) and family integration. The camp's empty days come with safety and with a dense community; the trade-off between the two settings runs through the day as much as through the budget.

Data, methods and reproducibility

Survey
391 households (257 in Zaatari, 134 in Irbid, Ramtha, Jerash, Zarqa and nearby towns), May to July 2016. Out-of-camp households were reached through the towns rather than through UNHCR files, so the sample includes families that registered-only surveys cannot see.
Design
Two-period household panel by recall (Syria before displacement, Jordan 2016). Pre-displacement values serve as balancing covariates and as placebo outcomes.
Estimand
Effect of living outside the camp for the households that did (ATT), under selection on pre-displacement observables.
Estimators
Entropy balancing on first moments (main); augmented inverse probability weighting; 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching on the propensity score with a 0.2 caliper; OLS. Robust and stratified-bootstrap standard errors. Rosenbaum bounds on matched pairs; Cinelli and Hazlett robustness values; Benjamini and Hochberg q-values across indices.
Conversions
Syrian pounds at a fixed pre-crisis rate (main), at purchasing power parity (welfare version, provisional GDP-level factors), and at the rate of the year each family left (thesis). Dinars at 1.405 USD.
Sample decisions
All 391 households in the file (the 2017 thesis used 384; the reason for the seven exclusions was not recorded). Syrian spending outside the camp is treated as item nonresponse: responders were richer and more often men, so the before-and-after of spending is reported for the camp only.
Limits
Overlap is thin (effective control sample of about 40 households); the minimum detectable effect is about 0.30 SD; subjective outcomes are answered by different people in the two groups; the exit-timing status proxy cannot be tested in this sample; pre-displacement values are recalled, and recall may differ between the groups; the map places households at their governorate of origin and town of interview, not at addresses.
Ethics and data protection
Before each interview, households were told the purpose of the study, which was purely academic, that nothing that could identify them would be asked, that they could decline any question or end the interview at any time, and that there was no reward for taking part and no consequence for declining. The household file holds no names, addresses or contact details. Participants are refugees, and in 2016 many Syrians outside the camps lacked valid residence documents, so this page publishes aggregates only: no household-level record is embedded in it, no statistic is reported for a subgroup of fewer than ten households, and flows on the map are drawn only for origin and destination pairs with at least five households. Places are given at the level of governorate of origin, town of interview and camp district. Open-ended answers appear only as coded categories, no respondent is quoted, and the people who helped reach households are not named. The survey was carried out for a master's thesis in 2016 and was not reviewed by an institutional review board; what is published here is a secondary analysis of de-identified data. The household file is kept encrypted and off line, and is not in the repository.

Every figure and table on this page is generated from three results files, one per module, produced by three scripts from the survey file; the numbers quoted in the text are taken from the same files.
