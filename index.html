A brief of the research · Syrian refugees in Jordan

Encampment, Self-Settlement and the Recovery of Displaced Households

In 2016, a Syrian household in Jordan lived under one of two support packages: a caravan and a voucher in Zaatari camp, or rent, tiered assistance and work in the towns of the north. A survey of 391 households, read again from the ground up, shows what each package let a household do, and what it did not.

By Felipe H. Rodríguez Villalta · Household survey, June to August 2016 · Research rebuilt and verified, October 2026

When hundreds of thousands of people crossed from Syria into Jordan, the question every host of a large displacement faces, where the households will live and under what rules, had two answers. One was Zaatari, the camp opened near Mafraq in July 2012, where a household received a shelter that became a caravan, water, schooling, free care in the camp's medical facilities and a food voucher of fixed value per person. The other was the towns, where most Syrians in Jordan lived: a household rented a dwelling, paid its electricity and water, worked mostly without a permit, and received assistance in tiers that depended on an assessment of need. In August 2015, when funding fell short, the voucher outside the camps was cut and part of the households assisted lost it; inside the camps the full value was kept.

This page summarizes a working paper that asks what each of those two support packages let a household do with what it had brought from Syria. It does not ask which place is better, and it does not estimate the effect of living in a camp: where a household ended up was not assigned. Until January 2015 the legal way out of Zaatari was a Jordanian sponsor, a route open to households with means and contacts, and a household that left without one could not obtain the Ministry of Interior card after July 2014. The households that reached the towns were, before the war, more often urban, more often savers and more often owners of a business. A comparison that ignores this selection compares what households brought as much as what they found.

The evidence is a survey of 391 Syrian refugee households interviewed between June and August 2016: 257 of them in Zaatari and 134 in the northern towns, mostly in Irbid and Ramtha. Each household also described its life in Syria before the war. The dots mark the origins in Syria and the places of interview in Jordan that the drawn lines join: four origins (Damascus and Rif Dimashq share one dot), the camp and four towns; the ten households interviewed in three other governorates are not on the map, nor are pairs below five households and the governorates that appear only in such pairs.
The camp households came above all from Daraa, the governorate just across the border: 211 of them, with 23 more from Damascus and Rif Dimashq and a thin line from Homs. The width of a line follows the number of households in the pair.
Most towns households came from the same border region, though less often than camp households (64% against 86% from Daraa governorate): the largest line to the towns runs from Daraa to Irbid, with Ramtha, Jerash and Zarqa behind, and thinner lines come from Damascus, Homs and Aleppo. A line is drawn only for pairs of five households or more, the rule of five of this page: 351 of the 380 households with a recorded origin; 11 recorded no origin. Counts are printed for two flows only, the two largest to the camp; the lines to the towns are read by their width.
The border region, enlarged: the city of Daraa lies about 10 km from Ramtha and 30 km from Irbid, and Zaatari sits inside Jordan, near Mafraq. Where a household ended up was not assigned; it followed what the household had brought and the rules of the time, which is where the comparison begins.

Chapter 1Two support packages

Inside Zaatari, in the summer of 2016, a household had a caravan in nearly every case, a connection to the camp's electricity network, schools run by the Ministry of Education, free care in the camp's medical facilities and the WFP voucher at its full value. The package changed around the fieldwork: a new grid that delivered nine hours of power a day came into service on 14 July 2016, during the fieldwork, and from the winter of 2016, after it, every family received cash for gas for heating and cooking, funded by UNHCR. Outside the camps a household rented (98% of urban refugees in a 2017 survey), paid electricity and water, and from November 2014 paid the rate of an uninsured Jordanian in the public health system. Work was mostly informal: in 2014 about one employed Syrian in ten held a work permit.

Assistance arrived in tiers. In August 2015, short of funds, WFP kept full assistance in the camps (USD 28.2 per person a month) and paid USD 14.1 to refugees in the communities it ranked in its highest-need category, half of the planned amount, and USD 7.05 to the others, who were excluded from September, when assistance to nearly 229,000 refugees in communities stopped. By February 2016 the voucher outside the camps ran in two tiers, JD 20 and JD 10 per person a month, and in 2017 UNHCR monthly cash reached about a quarter of registered families outside the camps. Jordan, under constraints of its own, hosted these households, and the Syrians in the country were more than one in eight of the people counted in its census of 2015; the assistance that reached both groups came from the organizations and from the state, which provided land, security and schooling in the camp and public schools and the public health system outside it. The comparison is between two support packages, not between a camp and a town as places and not between organizations and a state.

The camp sample reproduces the camp registry of 2015 on origin (86% from Daraa governorate against 82%), household size and the spread across the twelve districts; it holds more early arrivals than the registry and fewer households with work income than a national survey found in the camps in 2017 and 2018. The assistance households report matches the programme records: 93% of camp households name the voucher and nine in ten of them report exactly JD 20 per person; in the towns 86% name the voucher, 46% of them at the upper tier, above JD 12 per person (49% in the records), and 30% name UNHCR cash (26%). In economic terms the towns group resembles the national figures for refugees outside the camps, but it is not a probability sample: households were reached through one another, two thirds live in Irbid governorate, fewer than ten in Amman, and the households that left a camp with a sponsor weigh more in the sample than among all exits from Zaatari.

Chapter 2What the households brought

Households now in the towns differed before the war in liquidity and origin. Urban origin: 22% in the camp against 60% in the towns. Savings in Syria: 18% and 50%. A business: 37% and 65%. An origin in Daraa governorate, the border region: 86% and 64%. Recalled income in Syria hardly separates the groups (medians of 20 thousand pounds a month in the camp and 24 thousand in the towns): what differed was the form of the resources, not their level. These are the traits that, together with the rules of the time, decided where a household could live, and inside the camp the same traits still go with work today: among camp households that had savings in Syria, 64% report work income, against 30% of the rest.

Nine traits (seven from the household's life in Syria before the war: urban origin, savings, a business, a car, origin in Daraa, a pension and vacations; plus the household size and arrival by 2012) predict well where a household lives: the model has an area under the curve of 0.87 (0.5 would be chance and 1 a perfect separation), and urban origin, savings and a pension multiply the odds of living in the towns several times. Flexible models find no interaction the simple model misses, and six pre-war traits left out of the design barely predict the place, which is the closest thing to a placebo test the design can offer. Where a household ended up is a selection on what it brought.

Chapter 3Comparing like with like

How to read the tiers

Robust: the corrected p value is at or below 0.05, the AIPW estimate is at least 1.96 standard errors from zero, an unobserved trait would have to explain at least 5% of the residual variation (what the nine traits leave unexplained) of both the place and the outcome to take the difference to insignificance (the robustness value of Cinelli and Hazlett), and the five estimators share the sign. Null: the OLS p value is above 0.10 and the AIPW estimate is within 1.96 standard errors of zero. Fragile: everything in between. The reweighted camp is worth about 53 equally weighted households, so the comparisons are precise only for large differences: the smallest difference the design can detect is 20 to 25 points for a yes or no outcome. A robust difference is one that clears every check of this rule, no more: a second specification with eight traits (pension and vacations left out) moves income from work to fragile and leaves the other robust differences in place. Where a range is given for a difference, its ends are the two main estimators, AIPW and OLS (ordinary least squares with the traits as controls), except in the decomposition of the responses, whose two estimates are named where they appear. Even a robust difference is a difference among comparable households living under two packages; it is not the effect of moving a household from one place to the other.
Before any adjustment, the two groups differ beyond the survey margin on nine of the twelve outcomes. Towns households more often have income from work, hold a refrigerator and a stove and rate their security as good; camp households rate their integration, optimism and happiness higher, and spend half as much per person. Spending above income, debts and asking for assistance after a fall in income differ within the margin. These are all households, as they were found.
The working paper compares towns households with camp households that looked alike before the war: the average difference for the towns households, with the camp as the reference. Households whose estimated probability of living in the towns lies outside 0.1 to 0.9 have no comparable household on the other side and are set aside, as are the households with a pre-war trait missing; the comparison runs among the 116 camp and 95 towns households that remain, the common support.
Among those comparable households each difference is estimated with the doubly robust estimator (augmented inverse probability weighting, AIPW, consistent if either the model of the place or the model of the outcome is right), with an interval of 1.96 bootstrap standard errors; the bootstrap re-estimates everything on hundreds of resamples of the households, drawn within the strata of the sampling design (the camp districts and the towns). Four other estimators run as checks, and the twelve outcomes share a correction for multiple testing (Romano and Wolf).
Each difference carries a tier fixed before any estimate was read. Five are robust: income from work, spending per person, the refrigerator, the stove and self-rated integration. Two are fragile, debts and optimism. Five are null: spending above income, personal security, eating less after a fall in income, asking for assistance after it, and happiness.
Under a second specification with eight traits (pension and vacations left out), income from work moves to fragile and the other four robust differences stay; of the rest, debts moves to null, and personal security and eating less move to fragile. The estimates and their colours are those of the declared design; the note under a row gives its tier under the second specification. The tier is a verdict of the rule, not an effect of a place: even a robust difference is a difference among comparable households living under two packages.

The adjusted differences describe the towns households with a counterpart in the camp, 95 of the 134; the others, most of them the most urban and liquid households, lie outside every adjusted statement. The sections below read each part of the household budget with the descriptive figures first and the adjusted difference after.

Chapter 4What comes in, what has to be paid, and the gap

Assistance reaches nine households in ten in both groups, but as two different packages. In the camp the voucher is the income of most households: 93% name it, nine in ten of them at exactly JD 20 per person, and 64% of camp households report no income from work among their sources. In the towns the voucher comes in two tiers (median JD 10 per person), UNHCR cash reaches 30% of households, and 67% report income from work. Income per person is JD 20 a month at the median in the camp and JD 53 in the towns; assistance per person, JD 20 and JD 17.

Among comparable households the work gap is smaller than the raw one but it holds: towns households name work among their sources 24 points more often (32 points within the common support before adjustment, 31 among all households), with a corrected p value of 0.008 and the same sign in the five estimators. Under the towns package work comes into most households; under the camp package most households live on a floor of assistance. The amount of assistance per person is similar; what differs is what each household can add to it.

What the household has to pay differs more. In the towns 87% of households rent, at a median of JD 130 a month; fewer than ten camp households pay rent. Spending per person is JD 27 a month at the median in the camp and JD 56 in the towns, and among comparable households towns households spend 1.9 to 2 times as much per person, a robust difference. The gap itself does not differ: reported spending exceeds reported income in 45% of camp households and 50% of towns households, and among comparable households the difference of 9 to 17 points cannot be told from zero. Debts today are reported by 60% and 69%; the adjusted difference of 12 to 22 points clears only part of the tests. Both totals are minimums, because the questionnaire wrote down only what the household named, so the gap is read as a sign, not as an amount. Within the camp the gap falls on the households that live on the voucher alone: among camp households without work income, 58% report spending more than comes in, against 24% of those with work income.

In the camp, reported spending is food: 77% of the group total. Transport and other items are the only other entries that ten or more camp households reported; rent is paid by fewer than ten camp households, and the items reported by fewer than ten households are folded into the remainder.
In the towns the budget has two large items, rent (35% of the total) and food (33%), with electricity, water and transport behind them; education is reported by fewer than ten households. Both totals are minimums, because the questionnaire wrote down only what the household named.
Among the 104 towns households that rent and reported both income and rent, the thirds of the rent burden show the rest: in the lowest third, 63% ate less; in the middle and the highest third, nearly all did, and nearly all of the highest third carry debt. In the camp the gap is food against a fixed voucher; in the towns it is rent against income from work and tiered assistance, and eating less and borrowing rise with the rent burden.

Chapter 5When income fell

Income fell for 92% of camp households and 78% of towns households, and the two groups name different causes: in the camp, rising prices (152 of the 233 households that gave a cause), then the cut in assistance (42) and lack of work (39); in the towns, a cut in assistance (89 of 103), recorded without a date or a programme and most likely that of August 2015 in the voucher outside the camps. Among comparable households the shock is 21 points less frequent in the towns, a robust difference.
The survey asked which of eleven responses the household used at any time since arrival, from eating less to sending a child to work. The responses split in two. Loans and savings were more common among camp households: 68% of camp households asked for a loan against 32% in the towns, and 70% spent savings against 57%. Eating less and cutting other spending were more common among towns households: eating less, 55% of camp households and 73% of towns households; cutting other spending, 56% and 78%. A child went to work in 22% of camp households and 29% of towns households.
Among comparable households, towns households used savings 22 to 26 points less often (robust) and asked for a loan 19 to 21 points less often (fragile). Eating less and cutting other spending shrink: eating less to 7 to 15 points, which cannot be told from zero, and cutting other spending to 14 to 17 points, at the edge of significance under OLS and short of it under AIPW and the correction (fragile). The child at work is a difference the sample cannot tell from zero once households are compared like with like. Each range joins the AIPW and the OLS estimates, and the tiers belong to the shocks family of the design, fifteen outcomes with a correction of their own.
Each raw difference was then split, in a step declared before it was run, into the part due to the shock being more frequent in one group and the part due to how the households that faced it responded. Where the raw difference is larger in the towns (eating less, cutting other spending), the lower incidence of the shock there works against it: fewer towns households faced the fall in income, and those that did ate less more often than camp households in the same situation, by 20 to 21 points, and cut other spending more often, by 25 points. Each range here joins the two estimates of the decomposition: the difference given the shock computed with the weights of the design within the support, and the OLS among the comparable households with the shock, the one drawn.
The same towns households drew on savings less often, by 17 to 20 points, and on loans, by 20 to 24, and asked another member to bring income less often under OLS (16 points; 6 and within the noise under the weighted difference). The severe responses (cutting education, a child at work, selling belongings) do not differ. On the scale that WFP uses to grade responses, which counts cutting other spending as a crisis-level response, the towns households that faced the shock reached a further step on average under OLS (0.36 of a step; 0.26 and within the noise under the weighted difference): the mark of the spending cuts, not of the severe responses.
Four composite indices summarize the responses among all comparable households, not only those with the shock, so their figures are not those of the previous state: a difference among all comparable households mixes how often the shock came with how the households that faced it responded. Comparable towns households cut consumption on more fronts (0.2 to 0.3 more of the two cuts, a difference that does not clear the tests within the support) and drew on fewer financial buffers (0.3 to 0.5 fewer of the three: a loan, savings and asking for assistance; fragile), and, among all comparable households, no difference in the severe responses or in the most severe step reached on the scale that WFP uses to grade responses (from stress to crisis and emergency).
Read in the order the literature on household responses gives them, from the reversible responses (eating less, borrowing, drawing on savings) to the severe ones (cutting education, child work, selling belongings), neither group went further down the ladder than the other: the two groups do not differ in the severe responses, and the households with the shock differ in the reversible ones, eating less and cutting other spending in the towns, borrowing and drawing on savings in the camp.
Income fell for most households of both groups, for different reasons
Among the households whose income fell: under the camp package the gap was closed with credit and savings; under the towns package, with the plate, that is, by eating less and cutting what can be reversed. One reading, which the survey cannot test, is that neither pattern is a choice made freely: a household with a caravan and a voucher can borrow against a floor it will keep; a household paying rent out of tiered assistance has less to borrow against.

Chapter 6Health as a factor in the capacity to meet needs

A member with a medical condition is reported by 23% of camp households and 38% of towns households, a minimum, compared with about half of households outside the camps with a member diagnosed with at least one of five chronic conditions in a national survey of 2014; sickness among the shocks since arrival, by 20% and 18%. Health enters the analysis through these two yes or no items and nothing else: never crossed with a town, a district or a route out of the camp, never used to explain the scales. The question is whether, among comparable households and with the place held fixed, households with the item report work income more or less often, more spending above income, more debt, and more or different responses.

Households in which a member has a medical condition report work income, spending per person and spending above income close to those of the rest. What changes is how much they report having adjusted: among comparable households they used about 1.3 more of the eleven responses and drew more on their financial buffers (loans, savings, assistance), both beyond the correction for multiple testing over the 21 outcomes; loans, savings and asking for assistance are each higher on their own. Sickness among the shocks shows no gradient of its own beyond the correction. The gradient describes who found it harder to make ends meet; it is not an effect of health, since the condition is not randomly assigned and may precede or follow the fall in income. Health is a factor in the capacity of a household to meet its needs under either package: with incomes and spending like those of the rest, a household in which a member has a medical condition reports having done more to get by.

Chapter 7A refrigerator, a stove, and feeling at home

A displaced household leaves most of what it owned behind. More than nine in ten households of both groups had a refrigerator and a stove in Syria, so the two goods are read as basic living conditions reached in Jordan, the means to store and cook food at home, and not as wealth. A refrigerator is held by 44% of camp households and nearly all towns households; a stove by 23% and 74%. Among comparable households towns households hold a refrigerator 30 to 40 points more often and a stove 44 to 47 points more, the two largest differences among the yes or no outcomes of the main family, and both keep their tier under every check. The differences also reflect the conditions of each place: electricity (the camp's grid came into service during the fieldwork), space, and what is provided in kind.

Personal security is rated good or very good by 71% of camp households and 86% of towns households; the means coincide (4.1 out of 5) because camp answers sit at both ends of the scale, and among comparable households the difference of 8 points cannot be told from zero. Whom the household would turn to first in an emergency differs sharply: the police for 69% of camp households and 23% of towns households; family or relatives for 22% and 51%.

Self-rated integration is the largest difference of the whole study and the one with the highest robustness value: a mean of 8.0 in the camp against 4.8 in the towns, lower by 3.2 points among comparable households, robust under every check of the tier rule, and present among men and among women. The nine traits barely touch it: they add almost nothing to the regression of integration (the share of its variation that the regression explains, the R2, is 0.26 without them and 0.28 with them) and the adjusted gap equals the raw one, so the largest gap on this page is the one the nine traits explain least.
The question was brief and did not say integration into what; the answer is read as a sense of belonging or comfort where the household lives, and the shares at the ends of the scale stand beside the mean because in the towns few answers sit near the middle: 43% mark 1 to 3 and 37% mark 7 to 10. Optimism is higher in the camp by 1.1 to 1.7 points, a fragile difference concentrated among the women who answered; happiness differs by 0.5 to 0.9 of a point, which the sample cannot tell from zero.
The answer is about the same in Irbid, Ramtha and Jerash, so it is not a feature of one town; it is lower among households that left a camp informally, by another route or without saying how (4.0 against 5.7 among those that left with a sponsor) and among those whose income did not fall or gave no cause. In the camp the answer is high in every subgroup drawn, from 7.0 among the households that name lack of work as the cause of the fall in income to 8.4 among the later arrivals and among those that name the cut in assistance. The lower answers are consistent with the strain on the networks of Syrians in Irbid and the isolation outside Zaatari that other studies describe (Stevens 2016; Dajani et al. 2023).
Since women answered in 63% of towns households and 21% of camp households, part of the raw difference in the scales comes from who answered: with the camp's mix of men and women, happiness in the towns would be 5.0 instead of 4.5 and optimism 5.5 instead of 5.1, while integration would move 0.3 of a point, out of a gap of 3.2. The frame of reference may also differ: a camp resident may rate belonging among fellow Syrians in the camp, a town resident belonging among Jordanian hosts, and the survey cannot separate the two.
Self-rated integration: the largest difference of the whole study

The questionnaire ended with an open question on what an average day looks like. In the camp, "nothing" is the answer of 63% of respondents, men and women alike; working and watching sports follow among men. In the towns nobody answered "nothing": the day is taking care of the house (45%, the answer of 53% of the women who answered and 32% of the men) or sitting in the house (31%), and working for 15%. The two pictures describe the respondents of each group, mostly men in the camp and mostly women in the towns, so they are not compared across groups.

Chapter 8Over the years in Jordan

The survey is a single cross-section: it compares households that arrived in different years, it does not follow the same household over time. Households that have been in Jordan longer have more debt in both places (54% of camp households that arrived in 2013 or later against 69% of those that arrived by 2012; 60% against 80% in the towns) and hold more goods, a stove in the towns and a refrigerator in the camp. In the towns, those that arrived earlier report work income less often (55% against 78%); self-rated integration in the camp is lower among the earlier arrivals (7.5 against 8.4). Time in Jordan reads as accumulation on both sides, of goods and of debt; the survey cannot say whether any household's situation improved.

Chapter 9What the evidence supports, and what it cannot say

Households that could leave Zaatari, or never entered it, were more often those with liquid resources and urban ties before the war; where a household lives in Jordan is a selection on what it brought. Among households that looked alike before the war, households under the two support packages live differently in seven respects that clear every check of the tier rule (the ends of each range are the AIPW and the OLS estimates):

  • towns households more often have income from work (24 to 30 points);
  • they spend 1.9 to 2 times as much per person;
  • they hold a refrigerator (30 to 40 points) and a stove (44 to 47 points) far more often;
  • they faced a fall in income less often (21 to 25 points);
  • they used savings less often (22 to 26 points);
  • and their members rate their own integration about 3 points lower on a scale of 10, in Irbid, Ramtha and Jerash alike.

On the gap itself, on security and on happiness the two packages do not differ in ways this sample can detect; on debts, loans, cutting other spending, optimism and a few other responses the differences are fragile, and the eight-trait specification moves income from work to fragile. Under either package a household in which a member has a medical condition reports having done more to get by.

The camp package is a floor: a caravan, utilities, schooling, most health care and a voucher of fixed value, which most households live on; under it, when income fell, the gap was closed with loans and savings. The towns package is tiered assistance plus work, out of which the household pays a rent that takes close to half of its reported income at the median; under it a household more often has the basic living conditions and, when its income falls, closes the gap with the plate rather than with credit. Neither is a choice households made freely, and the data do not say which is better; they say what each one lets a household do.

A cut in assistance, most likely that of August 2015, made when the programme ran short of funds, is the cause of the fall in income that towns households name most often: a change in the funding of assistance is a change in the household budget. Jordan opened its doors to these households, and the assistance of the organizations and the state is the floor under both groups.

What the survey cannot say is as important. It did not ask which documents a household held, when and why it decided where to live, or whom it knew in Jordan; those are the traits the sponsor route selected on, and the design cannot hold them fixed. Employment and occupation describe the respondent; the amounts of spending are minimums; the responses cover the whole period since arrival; health is a minimum from two yes or no items; the towns group was reached through networks and does not stand for Amman; the data are one cross-section. Every adjusted difference reported here is a difference among comparable households living under two support packages in the summer of 2016, in a sample of 391, and the smallest difference the design can detect is about 20 to 25 points for a yes or no outcome. Nothing here is the effect of moving a household from one place to the other, and nothing here says which package is better.

Data, methods and protection

Survey
Designed and conducted by the author in June to August 2016 for a master's thesis at the University of San Francisco: 391 Syrian refugee households, 257 selected at random within the twelve districts of Zaatari camp and 134 reached by referral within groups set beforehand (a stratified snowball design) in the northern towns, by day and at home. 153 questions in 21 blocks; each household also described its life in Syria before the war.
Research design
Declared in writing before it ran. The average difference for towns households against camp households with the same nine pre-war traits, within a common support (0.1 to 0.9), under five estimators (AIPW as the main one; inverse probability weights, entropy balancing, bias-adjusted nearest-neighbour matching and OLS as checks), with a bootstrap that resamples households within the strata of the sampling design, a Romano and Wolf correction within each family of outcomes (twelve in the main family, fifteen in the shocks family), sensitivity analyses (Oster; Cinelli and Hazlett), minimum detectable differences, an eight-trait specification, five rules of inference, a double machine learning check in which no estimate leaves the parametric interval, and the reproduction of the thesis table. A decomposition of the responses into incidence and response given the shock, four composite indices and health as a factor were declared separately.
Verification
The main figures were counted a second time with separate code. Every family of results was replicated blind by a separate instance of the AI assistant that prepared the review, from the written declaration alone: the step 2 profile (909 values), the step 3 cuts (10,615), the main battery (330), the shocks family (350), the decomposition, indices and machine-learning check (581), health as a factor (1,769) and the step 5b cuts, all equal within rounding, with the Romano and Wolf p values differing by the bootstrap draws and never in tier. Further instances read each finished file, the technical note three times and the working paper, as a reader receives them.
Protection of the households
The household file and the scripts stay on the author's computer; only aggregates leave it, verified by checksum. No count below ten households is printed, and no count below ten can be worked out from what is printed: a cell reads "fewer than 10", "nearly all" (fewer than ten missing) or "withheld" when showing it would give another cell away, and where two printed figures together gave a count below ten, one of them was withheld. Three conventions say what is and is not such a count: the answering bases are printed in full where a table carries them, and neither the number of households that did not answer an item nor the traits of those households are treated as counts to protect, since a blank is not a characteristic of the household; a cell that is empty or full in a group (0% or 100%) is printed, since it describes the whole group and no household within it; and the map follows its own rule, stated in its caption. Five blind readings, two of the working paper and three of this page, tried the subtractions across tables that could recover a masked cell, and two further ones tried them across every sheet and file of the review; what they found was corrected, except the scales by subgroup of the step 3 workbook, which is not published until its next edition. Health enters through two yes or no items, never crossed with a town, a district or a route out of the camp. Open answers appear only as coded categories; no respondent is quoted. The people who helped reach households are not named. The map draws only Syria and Jordan, and its lines follow a rule of five households, stated under the map, because the data are few; counts on the map are printed for two flows only, the two largest to the camp, chosen so that no printed figure on the page, or in the workbooks of the review, fixes a count below ten when combined with them; a drawn line stands for five households or more, so a line may represent five to nine households, and no printed figure fixes that count. The survey had no institutional review; each household was told the purpose of the study, academic and nothing else, that it would not be asked anything that could identify it, that it could refuse any question or stop the interview, and that there was no payment for answering and no penalty for declining.
